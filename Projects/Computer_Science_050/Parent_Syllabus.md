## Course Purpose

Designed for beginners around ages 10–14, this course explains how computers work, how to solve problems by giving a computer instructions, and how to use artificial intelligence (AI) wisely. Students will practice creating small programs—sets of instructions that tell a computer what to do. The goal is to understand ideas, experiment, learn from mistakes, and make things that work; memorizing technical terms is not the goal.

Why study computer science when AI can write programs? AI can suggest answers and computer instructions, but students still need to explain what they want, understand what AI produced, spot mistakes, protect private information, and judge whether a result works. We will use AI as a learning assistant, not a replacement for thinking.

## The Two Programming Languages We Will Use

**Scratch:** A free, beginner-friendly programming language where students snap together colorful instruction blocks instead of typing commands. It makes programming ideas easy to see.

**Python:** A free, widely used programming language where students type instructions as text. We use it to create small quizzes, games, and other useful programs.

## What Students Will Be Able to Do

- Explain what a computer program does and break a simple problem into steps.
- Read, change, and write short Python programs that remember information, ask questions, make choices, repeat actions, and reuse instructions.
- Predict what code (program instructions) will do, test the result, and find and fix common mistakes (debugging).
- Complete and explain a small programming project.
- Recognize how computers store information, what major computer parts do, how the Internet connects computers, and how AI learns patterns from examples.
- Use AI critically: ask clear questions, check outputs, and avoid sharing sensitive information.

## How Teaching Works

We use circular (spiral) learning: students meet an idea briefly, see it again in a different activity, learn it in depth, and then keep using it. Major ideas appear at least twice before their main lesson. Every week combines explanation, reading short programs, hands-on practice, and a discussion of AI. Students also get brief introductions to topics such as how information is stored, how the Internet works, online safety, responsible online behavior, and computer-related careers.

## Weekly Schedule: Two Hours of Teaching + an Optional Third Hour

**Planned lesson (120 minutes):** Review, a short preview of future topics, teaching, guided practice, a hands-on activity, a short break, an AI connection, and a recap. All required material is planned for these two hours.

**Optional third hour (up to 60 minutes):** Open-ended questions, demonstrations, and side discussions chosen by student curiosity. We may occasionally run slightly past two hours, but essential lessons and progress checks will not depend on staying for this hour.

## 20-Week Course Outline

Weeks 1–3 use Scratch, where students build programs by connecting colored blocks. Week 4 compares that approach with Python, where instructions are typed. From Week 5 onward, Python is our main tool. Previously learned ideas appear again and again.

| Week | Weekly focus | Hands-on learning / AI connection |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is computer science? | Explore how computers follow instructions and respond to information. Try giving directions to a pretend robot and see a first Scratch program. |
| 2 | Programming with Scratch | Snap together blocks to make an on-screen character act. Preview keeping score, making choices, and repeating actions. |
| 3 | Step-by-step problem solving | Break a robot or game challenge into smaller steps. Revisit choices and repetition; practice noticing mistakes. |
| 4 | From Scratch blocks to Python text | Compare the same small program in both languages. Run Python to display a message and respond to a question. |
| 5 | Remembering information in programs | Create a scorekeeper or calculator. Use variables (named pieces of information), numbers, text, and simple calculations. |
| 6 | Making choices in programs | Build a simple quiz or choice game using if/otherwise rules; compare fixed program rules with AI suggestions. |
| 7 | Loops: repeating instructions | Repeat actions without rewriting the same instructions; predict results and learn to avoid endless repetition. |
| 8 | Functions: reusable instructions | Group several instructions under one name so they can be reused; make programs easier to read. |
| 9 | Functions that accept different inputs | Make one reusable instruction group work with different values; see how programmers use ready-made code. |
| 10 | Build a small Python program | Combine remembered information, choices, and repetition; briefly explore lists that hold several related items. |
| 11 | Finding and organizing information | Compare different ways to search for or sort everyday items, then practice earlier Python skills. |
| 12 | Testing and fixing mistakes | Read error messages, test surprising answers, check unexpected input, and see why AI-written programs need testing. |
| 13 | How computers represent information | Briefly see how 0s and 1s (binary digits) represent text and pictures made from tiny dots (pixels); practice Python. |
| 14 | Computer parts and saved programs | Meet the processor, working memory, storage, files, and folders. Practice saving and organizing programs. |
| 15 | How the Internet and websites work | Act out information traveling between computers. See where online AI runs and how webpages are built and made interactive. |
| 16 | How AI learns from examples | Try a small activity where AI sorts examples into categories. Discuss why examples matter and how AI creates text, pictures, or code. |
| 17 | Planning a useful program | Plan a project in everyday language before writing code; think about who will use it and how easy it will be to use. |
| 18 | Final project: build | Begin a small Python game, quiz, or helpful tool. The instructor will help keep the project manageable. |
| 19 | Final project: improve | Test the program, fix errors, and make it easier to use. Check any AI suggestions before accepting them. |
| 20 | Project showcase and reflection | Show the finished project, explain important instructions, review what was learned, and discuss when AI helped or made mistakes. |

### What Is Intentionally Repeated?

Students repeatedly practice breaking problems into steps, reading program instructions, finding and fixing mistakes, and checking AI suggestions. They also practice storing information (variables), making choices (conditions), repeating actions (loops), and grouping reusable instructions (functions).

### AI in Every Class

We will ask how each topic relates to AI, what an AI tool might get wrong, and how to check its answers. AI may help explain an idea or suggest ways to find a mistake, but students will first try core exercises themselves. AI use will be instructor-supervised; students do not need personal AI accounts.

## Materials and Home Involvement

No materials, personal devices, software, or homework are required. Lessons will be instructor-led, with demonstrations and interactive activities shared on a projector screen. At home, parents can encourage learning simply by asking what their child learned and inviting them to explain an interesting idea in their own words.

## Expectations and Progress

No previous programming experience is needed; activities can be adapted for different ages. Around Weeks 5, 10, 15, and 20, students briefly demonstrate what they have learned. The final project replaces an exam. No homework is assigned; advanced topics are saved for later courses.

**Course goal:** Students who can think through a problem, build and explain a small program, and use AI while keeping their own understanding and judgment.
