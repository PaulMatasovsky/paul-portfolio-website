## The Problem and Goal

I wanted to teach computer science to a mixed-age group, using explanations accessible to a 10-year-old without excluding teenagers or adults.

The goal is to give beginners a foundation in *how computers work and how to reason about programs*, not just teach them to copy code. The course connects computer science to familiar examples such as games, robots, everyday systems, and AI. It emphasizes predicting what a program should do, observing what it actually does, and explaining differences.

## My Role

I am the course organizer, curriculum decision-maker, lesson adapter, and instructor. I selected the audience and course structure, chose a Scratch-to-Python progression and spiral learning approach, and established recurring examples that help students connect new concepts to familiar situations.

AI supports much of the curriculum development and lesson preparation, but it is not the classroom instructor. My instructional work and verified student outcomes will be documented as evidence becomes available.

## Learning Progression

| Phase | Planned emphasis |
|---|---|
| Weeks 1–3 | Foundations, algorithms, input → processing → output, and instructor-led Scratch examples |
| Week 4 | Bridge from Scratch concepts to Python |
| Weeks 5–17 | Python and broader computer-science concepts, revisiting earlier lessons |
| Weeks 18–20 | Apply learning through a final project, review, and presentation |

Concepts are introduced, revisited, and reinforced rather than treated as mastered after one lesson. AI literacy and the need to verify AI-generated output recur throughout the course.

The optional lab is for experiments, questions, extra practice, and help for interested students or adults. All required instruction belongs in the two-hour core session.
