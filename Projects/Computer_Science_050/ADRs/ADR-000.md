# ADR-000: Teach Foundational Computer Science with AI Integrated Throughout

**Status:** Accepted  
**Recorded:** October 26, 2026 (retrospective; decision made during course design)  
**AI transparency:** AI helped research curriculum approaches, compare alternatives, and draft this retrospective ADR. I established the teaching goal and chose the course's direction. This record describes my decision; it is not evidence of completed classroom outcomes.

## Context

I am designing an introductory computer science course for students as young as 10, with older beginners and adults welcome. AI can already write code, explain concepts, and help solve problems. That raises a legitimate question: **Why should students learn computer science instead of concentrating on using AI tools well?**

Simply teaching programming syntax would not answer that question. But teaching AI prompts alone would leave students less prepared to judge whether a solution makes sense, identify errors, or understand what is happening behind the output.

The course has 20 weeks of two-hour core lessons, so it also needs an achievable introductory scope.

## Decision

**Teach foundational computer science, and connect those foundations to AI in every lesson.**

Students will learn to understand problems, create and read instructions, reason about data and program behavior, make predictions, test results, and debug mistakes. Scratch introduces these ideas visually; Python provides a way to apply them in typed programs. Broader computing concepts receive age-appropriate exposure without trying to become an advanced CS or IT course.

Each lesson includes a practical AI connection: what AI can help with, what it can get wrong, what students must understand for themselves, and how to evaluate what it produces. AI is taught as a powerful tool **within** computer science, not a replacement for understanding it.

## Alternatives Considered

1. **Focus mainly on AI tool use and prompting.** Valuable for immediate productivity, but insufficient for the course's goal of independent computational reasoning and evaluating generated programs.
2. **Teach conventional introductory programming with little AI discussion.** Stronger on basic coding, but misses the modern context that prompted the question and an opportunity to build habits of critical AI use.
3. **Combine CS foundations with recurring AI applications.** Chosen because it addresses both the underlying skills and their current applications.

## Research and Comparisons

During planning, I used AI to help compare established introductory curricula and educational guidance. These were reference points for judging scope and emphasis, **not** a claim that I independently validated each curriculum or that this course is officially aligned or accredited.

- **[CSTA 2026 PK–12 Computer Science Standards](https://csteachers.org/pk12standards/):** Define computer science more broadly than writing code or using software tools. Foundational areas include algorithms, programming, data, systems, and computing's impacts; AI is integrated across them rather than replacing them.
- **[Code.org AI Discoveries](https://code.org/en-US/curriculum/artificial-intelligence-discoveries):** A middle-school example of combining AI fluency with programming, data, websites, and creative computing projects. It provides a comparison for making AI a recurring part of the course rather than a single isolated lesson.
- **[AI4K12](https://ai4k12.org/):** A framework for age-appropriate AI concepts that reinforces the importance of explaining and evaluating AI, rather than treating a chatbot as a black box.

These sources support the *relevance and scope* of the choice. They do not prove this particular 20-week course's effectiveness; that must be evaluated through actual teaching and student understanding.

## Reasoning

My main objective is not that students memorize commands or become expert AI prompt writers. I want them to be able to **think through a problem, describe the logic, inspect a possible solution, and recognize when something is wrong**.

An AI system can help generate code. But a student who understands variables, conditions, algorithms, and testing is better equipped to ask precise questions, inspect its suggestions, troubleshoot errors, and use it responsibly.

I therefore chose to teach the fundamentals first, revisit them through spiral learning, and show how they apply to AI, robots, and a simplified fictional game. This approach also serves students who may later pursue programming, other technical fields, or simply become informed users of technology.

## Consequences and Tradeoffs

**Benefits:** The course remains relevant to contemporary AI while developing transferable computer-science reasoning. AI literacy, privacy, and verification are reinforced throughout rather than postponed to one unit.

**Tradeoffs:** Covering both foundations and AI within 20 weeks requires deliberate limits. Some topics will be introduced without requiring mastery. AI tools and examples will change, so individual lesson activities may need revision while the learning objectives remain stable.

The success of this decision will be judged by whether students can explain, predict, test, and improve simple solutions—not by the volume of AI-generated material or code they produce.
