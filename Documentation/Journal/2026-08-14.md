I am rebuilding my website from the ground up. This first Journal entry will summarize what has happened so far, and as such, it will be longer than normal.

1. How it Began
I started building a portfolio website with the simple goal of having an online place where I could display my résumé, technical skills, projects,
    and things I learned along the way, such as QA work.
My intent was to use this as a catalyst to change careers.

My initial focus was on getting the website structure built, and figuring out what a professional technical portfolio should contain. 
I used AI extensively because it made planning, learning, troubleshooting and producing content much faster than I could do natively. 
My goal was to build something useful, and I did that. I did not yet have a reason to question that approach.


2. Project Growth
I began developing my portfolio along with the site, and as I was looking for projects to create to add to my site, I was reminded of a previously
    completed a side project I had made to help print reports for my work.
It was not only something that I had completed, but it was something worth documenting and expanding.
Then I realized that the portfolio website itself was also worth showcasing.

I came across the concept of domain-driven design, and began applying that to the entire project, thinking about the different users of my 
    website: recruiters, hiring managers, other programmers. 
I started making deliberate architectural and organizational decisions. 
I decided to start using GitHub to display the work behind the the projects, including the website itself. 
The portfolio was becoming a record of how I work, and not just a collection of what things I had made.


3. Documentation
I realized that the finished project only told half of the story.
I also realized that without documentation, one could look at a project and not necessarily understand:
    Why I built it the way I did, what alternatives I considered, what problems I encountered, what I learned, or what decisions were mine as opposed to AI.

I began adding journals, product descriptions, and eventually ADRs. 
I wanted GitHub to demonstrate deliberate thought rather than just contain finished files. 
I started thinking more carefully about how documentation should look, where it should live, and how much is necessary. 
I came across the philosophy of Shibumi, or understated elegance, and began applying that across all my projects. 
Shibumi became increasingly useful as a guiding principle, and helped me to only create necessary documentation.


4. Authorship
AI had been heavily involved through much of this process.
It wasn't necessarily a problem, as I had intentionally used AI from the beginning because it made my work easier and helped me learn along the way.
My concern wasn't "Am I using too much AI?" My concern became, "When people look at my work, what can I confidently say demonstrates my abilities?"
Then other questions arose: "Is this actually how I talk or how I would explain something?" "Was something actually my idea and AI helped me implement it,
    or was it AI's suggestion and I just went along with it?" "What is really being displayed here: my creativity and skill, or AI's cleverness and my
    ability to copy/paste?"

I realized that these distinctions were not nearly as clear as I would like them to be, and were difficult to reconstruct after the fact. 
This created a problem specifically because the whole point to a portfolio is to demonstrate what I can do. 
Having a highly polished portfolio is not necessarily useful evidence if I can't clearly explain my contribution to it.


5. Starting Over
I had considered revising the content, or even using AI to help me untangle everything, but quickly dismissed them.
Editing AI-generated material until it sounded more like me would not completely solve the underlying authorship problem.
I could not go back and reliably determine the origin of every idea or decision.
I made the decision that a clear boundary would be both simpler and more authentic.
Website2.0 became that boundary.
I didn't need to throw away every last technical component, but I did need to reset the content and documentation.
also needed to establish rules about how, when, and where AI interacts with this project.
Starting over meant that from this point, I could now confidently explain how something was produced.


6. Corrections
What am I trying to correct with Website2.0?
I want to use AI for things such as: learning, brainstorming, questioning my assumptions, organizing my thoughts, technical assistance, critique and review.
I want to be able to clearly distinguish AI assistance from my own work.
I want to avoid presenting AI-generated polish as evidence of a skill I haven't actually demonstrated.
I further want to keep the portfolio focused on what I know, what I've built, what I'm learning, and how I think.
I want to do more of the actual writing myself.
I want to use AI where it makes sense to do so, and use it to it's full advantages.

7. Changing the way the project itself is managed
Instead of immediately rebuilding pages, I first figured out what the purpose of Website2.0 would be.
Then I got working on the architecture in a collaboration with AI.
We came up with a governing charter for AI to follow, which establish clearer rules for AI to follow, including: purpose, authorship, target audience,
    duplication, project scope, and AI usage.
We began formalizing ADRs so that important decisions and their reasoning aren't lost.
I reorganized documentation on GitHub so information has a logical home.
